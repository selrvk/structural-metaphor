# Deriving the metaphor

How to get from "here is a subject" to "here is the one idea", plus the ways
this step reliably goes wrong.

## The move

Categories give you clichés; specifics give you concepts. The reliable question
is not "what does a bakery site look like" but:

> **What does this subject have that its competitor down the road does not?**

Then: **is that thing an object, a place, a time of day, or a process?** Those
four shapes all extend naturally across a page, which is what you need — a
metaphor has to survive being applied to eight different slots.

- An **object** gives you parts (a laptop has a lid, a screen, apps, stickers).
- A **place** gives you space and depth (surface to seabed, street to rooftop).
- A **time of day** gives you a gradient and a natural direction of travel.
- A **process** gives you stages (dough proving, a roll of film developing).

If the distinctive detail is none of these — say it is an attitude, or a price
point — keep digging until you find its physical expression. "Great value" is
not a metaphor; the handwritten cardboard price signs in the window might be.

## Worked example: a burger stall

**Source material:** seven photos, three old reviews, a Facebook bio.

What the inventory turned up:

- Every venue photo was taken at dusk. Festoon bulbs strung across a gravel lot,
  palm trees, families at plastic tables. The light was doing all the work.
- The food photos were all shot on the shop's own branded greaseproof paper.
- An orange decal on the side of their truck read *"…we cook slow, but surely
  worth the wait."* The same sentence appeared in their bio and in the owner's
  own description. Three independent sources, same claim.
- Reviews repeated "best burger in town" and "big and juicy".
- They opened at 5PM.

**The distinctive detail:** the wait. They say it three times, in their own
words, unprompted. It is normally a liability; here it is the entire pitch.

**The metaphor:** one evening at the stall, 5PM to close. A time of day, so it
came with a direction of travel built in.

- Backdrop: a single gradient the length of the page, golden hour at the top
  through dusk to full night at the footer — which is exactly the gradient their
  own two venue photos already traced.
- Section dividers: strands of festoon bulbs slung between sections.
- Scroll indicator: a wire of bulbs across the top lighting up left to right as
  you descend. Evening falling, and a reading-progress cue in one gesture.
- Header: the illuminated lightbox sign from the side of their truck.
- 404: an unlit bulb, "Sold out na."

**Why it worked:** the palette was not chosen. Amber, dusk blue and red were
already in every photograph, so the page and the photos could not clash. And
"the wait" turned the one apparent weakness into the reason to come.

**The borrowed piece:** the menu section sat on their branded wrapping paper —
lifted from a rejected concept. The food photos were already shot on that paper,
so the images bled into the page. Keeping one element from a losing concept is
often the strongest move available.

## Worked example: an IT portfolio

**Source material:** a CV, a list of certifications, some project screenshots.

**The distinctive detail:** the laptop. Not glamorous, but it is the tool the
work is actually done on, and it is an *object* — so it has parts.

**The metaphor:** the person's laptop, rendered in 3D.

- Each section became a different app or feature of the machine.
- **Certifications became stickers on the back of the lid.** This is the move
  worth studying: a content type found a natural home inside the metaphor's
  object. Certifications are usually a boring list; as laptop stickers they are
  instantly legible as "things this person has collected", which is what a
  certification actually is.

**The transferable lesson:** after choosing a metaphor, go through the content
types one at a time and ask whether the object has somewhere for each to live.
Most will not. One or two will, and those are the ones people remember.

## Worked example: a subject with almost no material

Sometimes there are three photos and no reviews. The method still works, but the
inventory has to widen:

- What is the **physical process** of the work? A locksmith cuts keys; the
  jagged profile of a key is a divider, a section transition, a progress bar.
- What does the customer **hold** at the end? A receipt, a ticket, a paper bag.
- What is the **unit of the work**? A haircut is a session; a law firm's is a
  case file; a tutor's is a lesson.

If genuinely nothing is available, say so and choose restraint deliberately —
generous space, strong typography, no gimmick — rather than importing a metaphor
from nowhere. A stated decision to be plain is a concept. An accidental template
is not.

## Worked example: a SaaS product with no physical anything

The hardest case for a method built on *artifacts*: there is no shop, no light,
no wrapping paper. Screenshots of a dashboard are not artifacts — every
competitor has those.

**Where to look instead.** An intangible product still has:

- **A unit of work.** What does one use of it produce? An invoice, a resolved
  ticket, a merged branch, a shipped order. That unit is an object with parts.
- **A before/after.** What did the customer's day look like before? Spreadsheets,
  a whiteboard, sticky notes, a group chat. The thing being *replaced* is often
  more concrete and more emotionally loaded than the thing replacing it.
- **Its own vocabulary.** Every tool invents nouns for its concepts. Those nouns
  are the product's real artifacts.
- **The shape of the data.** A queue, a pipeline, a ledger, a calendar, a graph.

**Worked:** a small tool that reconciles supplier invoices. Distinctive detail
from the founder — customers describe the old process as *"the shoebox"*, an
actual box of paper receipts. The metaphor became the shoebox emptying: the page
opens cluttered with overlapping scanned-paper textures at slight angles and
resolves, section by section, into a clean ruled ledger by the footer. Scroll
indicator is a stack of receipts shrinking. The 404 is a receipt that fell
behind the desk.

**The transferable lesson:** when the product is intangible, the metaphor usually
lives in the *problem*, not the solution. Ask what the customer's desk looked
like before.

## Worked example: a fitness studio

Included because the cliché here is unusually strong and worth watching yourself
resist: black background, neon accent, slab caps, a word like GRIND or FORGE, a
looping video of someone lifting. It is a genuine style, executed everywhere,
and interchangeable across every gym in the world.

**What to ask instead:** who actually trains here? A studio whose members are
mostly women over 50 recovering from injury is a completely different subject
from one full of competitive lifters, and the category concept serves neither.

**Worked:** a small studio running one 6AM class and one 7PM class, six days a
week, same twelve people. The distinctive detail was not the equipment — it was
that the members knew each other's names and the whiteboard by the door still
had last week's scores on it in someone's handwriting.

The metaphor became **the whiteboard**: the page is a wall board, sections are
chalked panels, the class timetable is the board's own grid, and the scroll
indicator is a chalk line drawn down the margin. Content mapping: member results
become the scores column, in the handwriting the board already has.

**The transferable lesson:** for a category with an overwhelming visual cliché,
the useful question is not "how do I make this gym look different" but "who is
in the room". The cliché is designed for a customer this subject may not have.

## Worked example: a funeral home

The case where the method should almost switch itself off — and worth including
so that restraint reads as a decision rather than a failure to think of anything.

A structural metaphor applied to a funeral home is at serious risk of being in
poor taste. Anything cute is disqualifying. The scroll indicator should not be a
clever object. The 404 must not be a joke.

**What the method still gives you:** the inventory step, and the jobs. The jobs
are unusually clear and unusually urgent — someone using this page has had a
death in the family, often within the last few hours. They need a phone number,
what happens next, and what it costs. Everything else is decoration on someone's
worst day.

**Worked:** the distinctive detail was that the family had run the home for
three generations and still walked every procession on foot. The concept was
**pace**: generous spacing, one idea per screen, nothing that moves, a page that
does not hurry. The eight slots are applied at the lowest possible amplitude —
the "metaphor" is the whitespace and the unhurried rhythm, and the phone number
is fixed and reachable from every point on the page.

**The transferable lesson:** the eight slots have a volume knob. Some subjects
want it near zero, and turning it down deliberately — and saying why — is a
concept. It is not the same as having no idea.

## Worked example: a bespoke tailor

The richest content-mapping case, and a good one to study for step 4.

**The inventory** is unusually full of artifacts: chalk marks, brown pattern
paper, a tape measure, pins, a half-made jacket on a stand, the label sewn into
a finished lining. Any of them could carry a page.

**The distinctive detail** was the pattern paper: every customer has a paper
pattern cut to their body, kept on a rack at the back, reused every time they
order again. Some had been on the rack for thirty years.

**The metaphor:** the page is a pattern piece. Backdrop is brown pattern paper,
section edges are cutting lines with notches, dividers are tailor's chalk, the
scroll indicator is a tape measure running down the margin in real units.

**Content mapping — three separate hits, which is unusual:**

- The process (measure → cut → first fitting → finish) becomes the section order,
  because that *is* the customer's journey.
- Testimonials become labels sewn into linings.
- The price list becomes a cutting list, itemised the way a tailor already
  itemises cloth, canvas and buttons.

**The transferable lesson:** when a subject is artifact-rich, resist using all
of them. One metaphor, applied to the eight slots, plus one or two content maps.
Chalk *and* pins *and* tape *and* labels *and* paper is the costume problem.

## Anti-patterns

**The costume.** The metaphor is applied to the content as well as the chrome
until the page is exhausting — every heading in a "handwritten" font, every box
a torn paper edge. Confine it to the eight slots.

**The category concept.** "It's a bakery, so: flour texture, kraft paper, a
rolling pin." True of every bakery. If the concept would survive swapping in a
competitor's logo, it is not a concept.

**The metaphor that fights the assets.** A dark, moody idea over bright daylight
photos, or a minimal white concept over low-resolution snapshots that need
cropping and treatment to look intentional. Check the photos before committing —
the "Source fit" line in the concept template exists for this.

**The idea that obstructs the job.** An intro animation before the phone number.
A scroll-jacked narrative on a page whose job is showing prices. If the metaphor
and the job disagree, the job wins every time.

**The invented detail.** Reaching for a metaphor and quietly inventing the fact
that supports it — a founding year, a family story, a "secret recipe". If it is
not in the source material it does not exist, however good the concept would be.

## Starting points by category

Deliberately thin. These are for when you are stuck, not answers — push past
them once you have read the actual source material.

- **Restaurant / food** — courses as sections; the table as canvas; heat and
  steam as transitions; the wrapper the food is served in
- **Cafe** — the page as a drink, light at the top darkening to espresso at the
  footer; scroll fills a cup; sections as a stamped loyalty card
- **Bakery** — proving and rising as scroll behavior; tray layers; parchment
- **Portfolio / freelancer** — the tool of the trade as an object; the workspace;
  a project as a case file
- **Developer / technical** — the machine, the editor, the terminal, the build
  pipeline. Note this is the category cliché — use it only if the person is
  specific about *which* machine or *which* workflow
- **Resort / hotel / beach** — depth: sunlit surface darkening as you scroll;
  tide going out; sand at the footer
- **Barbershop / salon** — the pole; mirror panels; folded fabric; slow soft
  transitions
- **Auto / mechanical** — the dashboard, gauges, a tachometer sweep, brushed
  metal and warning-light amber
- **Hardware / construction** — blueprint grid; spec-sheet panels; measuring-tape
  dividers; a stamped approval block
- **Medical / dental / legal** — restraint *is* the concept. Light panels, calm,
  no gimmick. Trust is the deliverable
- **Utility / logistics** — clean labeled containers; big legible numbers. The
  pricing *is* the design
